The score is blind to mixture weights

A two-mode target: p(x) = π·p₁(x) + (1−π)·p₂(x), with π of the mass on the left. Langevin dynamics is driven only by ∇ₓ log pσ(x). Watch what the particles do as you change the noise level σ.

2 · The score ∇ₓ log pσ(x)

The grey dashed curve is the score of the same mixture but with π = 0.5. At small σ the two curves lie exactly on top of each other everywhere that matters — the weight is invisible to the score. Raise σ and they separate.

 

3 · Why: the responsibility r₁(x)

The score is a responsibility-weighted average of the two local scores: ∇log pσ = r₁·(μ₁−x)/v + (1−r₁)·(μ₂−x)/v. π enters only through r₁. At small σ, r₁ is a hard step — it is 1 or 0 no matter what π is, so π cancels. Noise turns the step into a ramp whose height carries π.

What to try

  1. Leave σ at 0.05 and hit Reset. Particles start spread out, fall into whichever mode is nearest, and freeze. The black marker shows the target of 0.80 — the bar sits near 0.50. The modes are perfect, the weights are wrong, and crossings/1k steps is essentially zero. This is the pitfall.
  2. Drag σ up to ~2.5. The valley fills in, the two score curves visibly separate, r₁ becomes a smooth ramp, and the bar slides to 0.80. Correct weights — but the density is now a single blurred blob, not the data.
  3. Hit "Run annealed Langevin". σ walks down a geometric ladder. The large levels settle which mode and in what proportion; the small levels sharpen the shape. You end with both the right weights and the right modes — which is the whole point of the noise schedule.
  4. Push π to 0.95 at σ = 0.05 and watch the score panel: the solid and dashed curves still overlap. A 19:1 mass ratio and a 1:1 mass ratio produce literally the same gradient field.
